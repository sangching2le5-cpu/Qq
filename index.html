Lesson A

Disasters and tragedies

Natural disasters and disasters caused by people — vocabulary, pronunciation, and the verbs we use with them.

Learning objectives

  • Name 18 types of disaster and tragedy in English and pronounce the difficult ones correctly (drought, volcano, famine, tornado).
  • Use injure with people and damage with things.
  • Use the verbs erupt, spread, starve, shake, suffer, break out, sweep through in natural sentences.
  • Explain what be feared dead means in a news report.

1 · Lead-in

Read this news sentence: “A big earthquake shook the city at noon today.” Which other disasters can you name in English? Write down five before you continue.

Show a suggested answer

flood, drought, volcano, famine, landslide, epidemic, hurricane, tornado, typhoon, war …

2 · Vocabulary presentation

Natural disasters (not caused by human beings)

Disasters and tragedies caused by people

Verbs and phrases connected with these words

3 · Explanation

injure vs damage

Injure /ˈɪndʒə/ is used about people. Damage /ˈdæmɪdʒ/ is used about things and property.

Compare: 200 people were injured. Many buildings were damaged.

Common mistake: ✗ “Many buildings were injured.” ✓ “Many buildings were damaged.” — Buildings cannot be injured.

The verbs in the original examples

erupt /ɪˈrʌpt/ — used about a volcano: hot rock and gases come out. A volcano has erupted in Indonesia.

be feared dead /bi fɪəd ded/ — a news-report phrase: people think (but do not yet know for sure) that these people are dead. Hundreds are feared dead.

spread /spred/ — to move and reach more places or people. The past form is the same: spread – spread. The flu epidemic spread rapidly throughout the country.

starve /stɑːv/ — to suffer or die because there is not enough food. Millions are starving as a result of the famine.

shake – shook – shaken /ʃeɪk – ʃʊk – ˈʃeɪkən/ — to move suddenly and violently. A big earthquake shook the city at noon today.

suffer /ˈsʌfə/ — to experience something bad. The area is suffering its worst drought for many years.

break out /breɪk ˈaʊt/ — used about war, fighting, disease or fire: to start suddenly. Break out – broke out – broken out. Civil war has broken out in the north of the country.

sweep through /swiːp θruː/ — to move quickly and with force through a place. Sweep – swept – swept. A tornado swept through the islands yesterday.

Grammar pattern to remember: a disaster + verb — “A volcano has erupted.” · “Civil war has broken out.” · “The epidemic spread rapidly.” Use the present perfect when the news is recent and the result is still important now.

4 · Examples from the textbook

  • A volcano has erupted in Indonesia.
  • Hundreds are feared dead.
  • The flu epidemic spread rapidly throughout the country.
  • Millions are starving as a result of the famine.
  • A big earthquake shook the city at noon today.
  • The area is suffering its worst drought for many years.
  • Civil war has broken out in the north of the country.
  • A tornado swept through the islands yesterday.
  • 200 people were injured. Many buildings were damaged.

5 · Check your understanding

6 · Section review

  • Natural disasters: earthquake, flood, drought, volcano, famine, landslide, epidemic, hurricane, tornado, typhoon, tropical storm.
  • Disasters caused by people: explosion, major accident, incident, plane crash, war, civil war.
  • injure → people; damage → things.
  • Key verbs: erupt, spread, starve, shake (shook, shaken), suffer, break out, sweep through.

Lesson B

People involved in disasters and tragedies

casualty · victim · survivor · refugee · migrant · the dead · the wounded

Learning objectives

  • Use the seven words for people correctly and count them properly (casualties, refugees …).
  • Explain the difference between a casualty, a victim and a survivor.
  • Explain the difference between a refugee and a migrant.

1 · Lead-in

A building collapses in an earthquake. Some people die, some are hurt, some walk out alive, and some have lost their homes. How many different English words do you need to talk about all of these people?

Show a suggested answer

the dead, the wounded, casualties, victims, survivors — and, if they must leave their country, refugees or migrants.

2 · Vocabulary presentation

3 · Explanation — what is the difference?

casualty — a person who is killed or injured in an accident or war. It counts both the dead and the injured: “300 casualties” can mean 200 dead and 100 injured.

victim — a person who suffers as a result of something bad. Victims are not always physically hurt: “The real victims of civil war are children left without parents.”

survivor — a person who lives through a disaster when other people die: “There were only three survivors. All the other passengers were reported dead.”

refugee — a person who has escaped from their own country because of a war or another disaster: “Thousands of refugees have crossed the border looking for food and shelter.”

migrant — a person who travels from one country to another, often to find work or a better life. Unlike a refugee, a migrant is not necessarily escaping danger: “Millions of migrants enter the country each year, looking for a better life.”

the dead / the wounded — adjectives used as plural nouns: “the dead and wounded were flown out in helicopters”. Wounded means injured in a battle or by a weapon.

Grammar notes
• Countable: a casualty, casualties; a victim, victims; a survivor, survivors; a refugee, refugees; a migrant, migrants.
• Plural only with the: the dead, the wounded — “The wounded were taken to hospital.” (never “a wounded”)
• Prepositions: a victim of something, a survivor of a crash, refugees from a war, migrants into / to a country.

4 · Examples from the textbook

  • The explosion resulted in 300 casualties.
  • The real victims of civil war are children left without parents.
  • There were only three survivors. All the other passengers were reported dead.
  • Thousands of refugees have crossed the border looking for food and shelter.
  • Millions of migrants enter the country each year, looking for a better life.
  • During the battle, the dead and wounded were flown out in helicopters.

5 · Check your understanding

6 · Section review

  • casualty = dead + injured (in an accident or war).
  • victim = anyone who suffers the results.
  • survivor = someone who lives through a disaster.
  • refugee = someone who has escaped their country because of war or disaster.
  • migrant = someone who moves to another country, often for work.
  • the dead / the wounded = plural groups, always with the.

Lesson C

Diseases and epidemics

rabies · AIDS · HIV · malaria · cholera · typhoid — plus the words we use to talk about how diseases spread.

Learning objectives

  • Name six diseases and say how each one is transmitted.
  • Explain clearly that HIV is the virus and AIDS is the illness.
  • Understand four authentic English newspaper headlines about disease.

1 · Lead-in

Look at this headline: “Rabies out of control in many parts of Asia.” What does out of control mean? Which disease would you be most worried about in a hot country with poor water supplies?

Show a suggested answer

Out of control = nobody can stop it. In a hot country with poor water supplies, cholera and typhoid are the biggest danger because they spread through contaminated food and water.

2 · Vocabulary presentation — the diseases

Words that help you talk about diseases

3 · Explanation — be accurate

HIV is not the same as AIDS. HIV (human immunodeficiency virus) is the virus. AIDS (acquired immune deficiency syndrome) is the serious illness that can develop later because HIV damages the immune system. A person can have HIV for many years without having AIDS.

Malaria is transmitted by the bite of an infected mosquito. It is not spread from one person to another by touch or by the air.

Cholera and typhoid are often spread through contaminated food or water — that is, food or water that carries the bacteria.

Rabies can be transmitted through the bite or the saliva of an infected animal, for example a dog or a fox. It is a very serious disease.

Note: this lesson teaches vocabulary, not medical advice. If you need medical information, ask a doctor.

4 · Examples — newspaper headlines

  • Rabies out of control in many parts of Asia.
  • New AIDS unit to be opened this month.
  • New malaria drug tested.
  • Cholera and typhoid injections not needed, says Tourism Minister.

Headlines often drop small words. “New malaria drug tested” = “A new malaria drug has been tested.” “New AIDS unit to be opened” = “A new AIDS unit is to be opened.”

5 · Check your understanding

6 · Section review

  • rabies — from the bite or saliva of an infected animal.
  • HIV / AIDS — HIV is the virus; AIDS is the illness.
  • malaria — from the bite of an infected mosquito.
  • cholera / typhoid — often from contaminated food and water.
  • epidemic — a disease affecting large numbers of people.
